*Part five of seven. The argument so far has concerned whether a nation may order its public life in acknowledgment of Christ, and what that acknowledgment cannot be made to mean. This week the question narrows to a standard. If civil government is accountable to something beyond its own will, what is that standard, and what sort of political order would be required for it to govern anything at all?*

## Theonomy and Christian Nationalism: A Relationship Worth Clarifying

Another conviction I brought away from the conference concerns the relationship between theonomy and Christian nationalism.

Theonomy, broadly understood, holds that God's law provides the ultimate standard of justice and that civil government is not exempt from God's moral authority. The precise application of Old Testament law to contemporary civil government is a substantial theological question, and theonomists themselves do not agree on every detail.

Christian nationalism, likewise, has multiple definitions. For purposes of my argument, I am using the term to describe the conviction that a nation should acknowledge its Christian character and order its public life, including its civil institutions, in recognition of Christ's authority.

With those definitions in view, I believe that **theonomy requires a Christian-national framework if it is to become the governing principle of civil law.**

By this, I mean a comprehensive theonomic vision in which God's revealed law is acknowledged as the authoritative standard for civil justice—not merely a political order that happens to enact individual laws consistent with biblical morality. Theonomy makes a claim about the standard by which civil law should be measured. If God's law is to serve as that governing standard, the political community must, in some meaningful sense, acknowledge the authority of the God who gave it.

A society that formally excludes Christianity from its understanding of public authority cannot consistently implement a comprehensive theonomic vision. It may adopt individual laws that happen to agree with biblical standards, but that is different from recognizing God's law as the authoritative standard for civil justice.

In that sense, I see Christian nationalism, as I have defined it here, as the political framework within which comprehensive theonomy could be pursued. This does not mean that every form of Christian nationalism is theonomic, that every Christian-nationalist position agrees on the application of biblical law, or that the label itself resolves the theological questions involved.

This does not settle the difficult questions. Which Old Testament laws continue to apply to civil government? How should we distinguish enduring moral principles from laws tied specifically to Israel's covenantal polity? What role should the New Covenant play in interpreting those laws? How should civil authority be exercised without confusing the magistrate's responsibilities with those of the church?

These are not peripheral details. They are central theological questions that require careful biblical and confessional reasoning.

But the difficulty of answering them does not eliminate the underlying issue: If Christ is Lord over civil government, then civil government must be accountable to a standard beyond its own will. Theonomy attempts to identify that standard in God's revealed law. Whether a particular theonomic proposal faithfully identifies and applies that law must be established through careful exegesis and theological argument, not assumed merely because the proposal invokes Scripture.
