There is a particular kind of grief that visits the Christian who has prayed for healing, done everything he was told to do, and remained sick. He has confessed every sin he can remember. He has forgiven every person who ever wronged him, real or imagined. He has spoken the words of faith rather than the words of doubt. He has claimed the promise, resisted the spirit of infirmity, and refused to "make room for unbelief." And still, the disease progresses. Still, the pain remains. Still, the body he prayed over is buried.

What happens next is often worse than the suffering itself. He begins to search for the reason the formula failed. Was there a root of bitterness he missed? A generational curse unaddressed? A measure of faith too small? The theology he was given left him only one place to put the blame: himself.

This article is written against that theology—not against the desire for healing, which is good and biblical, but against the idea that healing operates by a discoverable, repeatable spiritual mechanism. The thesis is simple and, I think, unassailable from Scripture: **God heals because He is sovereign, not because we have discovered a spiritual formula.** He may heal miraculously today, and Christians should pray boldly and expectantly that He will. But healing is never owed, never mechanical, and never guaranteed by any combination of forgiveness, confession, spoken words, or ritual. It is, every time, an act of unmerited sovereign mercy.

## The God Who Does Whatever He Pleases

Any doctrine of healing must begin, as all Christian doctrine must begin, with the character of God. Isaiah 46 gives us one of the most direct statements of divine sovereignty in the whole of Scripture. The prophet contrasts the idols of Babylon—carried, carted, and set in place by their own worshipers because they cannot move themselves—with the living God who carries His people. Then comes the declaration:

> "Remember this and stand firm... I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done, saying, 'My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose'" (Isaiah 46:9–10).

Notice what is being claimed. Not merely that God can do what He wills, but that His purpose *will stand*—it cannot be thwarted, negotiated, or triggered into motion by human technique. The chapter is not about healing specifically; it is about the entire scope of God's rule over history, nations, and idols. But that is precisely the point. Healing is not a special category exempt from this sovereignty. It falls under the same "counsel of his will" that governs empires. If God's purposes for the rise and fall of Babylon are not subject to human manipulation, neither are His purposes for a diagnosis.

Daniel 4 makes the same point from a different angle—not the sovereignty of God in the abstract, but the sovereignty of God demonstrated against a proud king who had to be personally, painfully humbled to learn it. Nebuchadnezzar's madness and eventual restoration culminate in his own confession:

> "He does according to his will among the host of heaven and among the inhabitants of the earth; and none can stay his hand or say to him, 'What have you done?'" (Daniel 4:35).

Paul crystallizes both texts into a single doctrinal statement in Ephesians 1:11, describing believers as those "predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will."

This is the theological bedrock beneath everything that follows: healing, when it happens, happens because God wills it, not because a person has assembled the right combination of inputs.

## What James 5 Actually Says—and Does Not Say

No text is more frequently pressed into service as a healing formula than James 5:14–15:

> "Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up. And if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven."

Read carefully, in context, this passage does not teach a formula. It teaches a pattern of pastoral care and prayer *offered to* a sovereign Lord, not *imposed upon* Him.

Several details matter.

- The healing is explicitly attributed to **the Lord**, not the ritual.
- James says **"if he has committed sins,"** refusing to assume sickness always results from personal sin.
- The entire letter emphasizes submission to God's will rather than presumption upon it (James 4:15).

James 5 calls the church to pray confidently while leaving the outcome in the hands of God.

## When the Answer Is No

The New Testament also gives us explicit examples of faithful believers who were **not** healed.

Paul's thorn in the flesh (2 Corinthians 12:7–9) stands as the clearest example. Three times Paul pleaded for its removal, yet God's answer was:

> "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness."

Paul cannot be accused of insufficient faith, hidden sin, or poor technique. God's refusal served a gracious purpose.

Likewise:

- Paul left **Trophimus sick at Miletus** (2 Timothy 4:20).
- Paul advised Timothy to use **ordinary medicine** for his stomach ailments (1 Timothy 5:23).

Even the apostles did not treat healing as something available on demand.

## The Refusal of Jesus to Standardize His Own Miracles

If healing followed a formula, we would expect Jesus to model it consistently.

Instead, the Gospels show remarkable variety.

Sometimes He healed with a word.

Sometimes with touch.

Sometimes with mud.

Sometimes from a distance.

Sometimes immediately.

Once in two stages.

Sometimes because of another person's faith.

Sometimes when no faith is mentioned at all.

Sometimes even among widespread unbelief.

The diversity appears intentional. Jesus refused to reduce divine healing to a reproducible technique.

## Testimonies Encourage; They Do Not Legislate

None of this denies that God still heals.

He does.

Christians should pray boldly and confidently for healing.

But testimonies are not doctrine.

A testimony tells us **what God did**.

It cannot tell us **why** He did it, nor establish a universal rule for what He must do next time.

Scripture—not experience—is the church's final authority.

This is why formula theology becomes so dangerous. It elevates experience into binding revelation and inevitably leads suffering believers to wonder what they did wrong when healing doesn't come.

Job's friends made precisely this mistake.

God rebuked them.

## Resting in Sovereignty While Praying with Boldness

So what should Christians do?

Pray.

Call the elders.

Ask boldly.

Use ordinary means such as physicians and medicine without embarrassment.

Trust God's wisdom whether He heals immediately, gradually, through medical care, or not until the resurrection.

Most importantly, refuse the temptation to audit your own faith looking for the hidden reason healing did not come.

Sometimes suffering remains.

When it does, Scripture teaches us not to search for a missing formula, but to rest in the God who works **all things** according to the counsel of His will.

The God who declared the end from the beginning, humbled kings, and governs every detail of history is the same God who hears every prayer for healing.

He does not owe us a formula.

He has given us Himself.
