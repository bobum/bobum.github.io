*Part four of five. "Just be like Jesus." The phrase is true, it is necessary, and it ends more conversations than it starts. I have no interest in weakening that summons. But it carries a distinction that is easy to lose, and losing it is how we arrived at the problem this series has been describing.*

## Be Imitators of Me, as I Am of Christ

The summons itself is not in question. Ephesians 5:1–2 tells us to be imitators of God as beloved children and to walk in love as Christ loved us. 1 Peter 2:21 says Christ left us an example, that we should follow in his steps. 1 John 2:6 says whoever claims to abide in him ought to walk as he walked. Nothing argued so far in this series diminishes that calling.

The distinction is this. Christ's unique redemptive office, his work as the incarnate Son who atones for sin, who exercises divine authority, who fulfills messianic prerogatives given to him alone as the Mediator between God and man, is not something we imitate or replicate. We are not mediators. We do not atone. We do not speak with Christ's underived divine authority when we confront error. What we imitate is his moral character, and we imitate it as finite, sinful creatures being progressively sanctified by grace. The office is not ours.

That is why Paul's own words help us here. Paul can say, with no tension at all against Christ's supremacy, "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ" (1 Corinthians 11:1). He does not set himself up as a rival standard. He offers himself as a servant in whom Christlike faithfulness has taken visible, ordinary, human shape. And the shape it takes in Paul's life is not emotional flatness. It is boldness before governors and kings. Doctrinal precision defended at real personal cost. Erring believers and false teachers confronted by name. Perseverance through beatings and shipwreck and imprisonment. Zeal running through all of it. Because the apostles were ordinary human servants and not the divine Son himself, they show us what Christlike faithfulness looks like when it is lived out by people like us. If you want to know how zeal for Christ is meant to be expressed in a human life without divine prerogative, this is where Scripture points you.

All of it has to be anchored somewhere, and Scripture is the only candidate. Not personality. Not temperament. Not the shifting expectations of the surrounding culture, and certainly not whether we are perceived as nice. So the question is never whether something feels gentle to my neighbor, or whether it would be well received in polite company. The question is whether my zeal is being shaped and governed by what God has revealed. Zeal cut loose from Scripture degenerates into self-righteous aggression. Gentleness cut loose from Scripture degenerates into cowardice dressed up as virtue. Only the Word rightly governs both.

## The Domesticated Christ

So here is the charge, stated plainly, because I believe it is true and the church needs to hear it. We have taught generations of Christians that being Christlike means being perpetually soft-spoken, endlessly agreeable, emotionally restrained, and allergic to conflict. Meanwhile Christ's prophetic denunciations in Matthew 23, his zeal-driven cleansing of the temple, and the apostles' extraordinary boldness through Acts and the epistles have been quietly set aside. That is not the whole biblical picture of Christ. It is a domesticated Christ, shaped more by modern therapeutic and social expectations of niceness than by the actual text of Scripture. And when believers formed by that Christ grow anxious and apologetic about their own God-given convictions, we should not pretend to be surprised.

The other side of this conversation deserves fairness, because the concern behind it is real and biblical rather than a strawman. Christians who warn against sinful anger, spiritual pride, cruelty in speech, and reckless, undisciplined confrontation are warning about something Scripture warns about. Much of what passes for "boldness" online today really is unsanctified anger wearing zeal's clothing. The problem is not that gentleness gets preached. The problem is when "gentleness" quietly becomes a euphemism for never confronting error, never naming sin as sin, never calling false teaching false, never raising one's voice under any circumstance, and never letting anyone feel the discomfort that truth sometimes produces. By that point it has stopped being a fruit of the Spirit. It has become a strategy for avoiding cost.

There is a cost to all of this, and it falls in two places: on the person who tells the truth and is resented for it, and on the person who has been told his conviction is anger so often that he has begun to believe it. **Next week, in the final part: offense, exhaustion, and the zeal I want back.**
