*Part four of seven. Last week I argued that a substantially Christian people will naturally come to express its convictions in its public life. That argument is worth very little if it cannot be distinguished from a very different claim, often made in the same vocabulary, that a particular ancestry carries a superior standing before God. The distinction cannot be made by assertion alone, so I want to state it carefully.*

## Christian Nationalism Is Not Ethnic Supremacy

One objection to Christian nationalism is that it can become entangled with ethnic nationalism, treating a particular ancestry as though it confers a superior claim to a nation or to God's favor. That danger is real, but it is not inherent in the conviction that a nation should acknowledge Christ.

I do not believe any tribe possesses an inherent spiritual superiority over another. No bloodline purchases a place before God, and no ancestry gives anyone a claim on the mercy seat. We are all sinners in need of the same grace, and salvation is found in Jesus Christ rather than in our genealogy.

The church itself testifies to this reality. Christ is gathering a people from every tribe, tongue, people, and nation. Our unity in Him does not depend on shared ethnicity, and no earthly ancestry places someone closer to the throne of grace.

That conviction should shape how Christians think about the nation. If a nation is to be Christian, its defining characteristic should be its acknowledgment of Christ and its commitment to ordering public life according to Christian convictions—not the supposed superiority of a particular bloodline.

This does not mean that national borders, citizenship, cultural inheritance, or a government's responsibility toward its own population are meaningless. Those are legitimate subjects for political and theological discussion. But they must not be turned into claims of racial superiority or hereditary spiritual privilege.

Christianity cannot be reduced to an ethnic inheritance. One can possess a Christian ancestry without possessing Christian faith, just as someone from a people with no historical Christian majority can be brought into the kingdom through the gospel.

The relevant question is not which bloodline has the strongest claim to a nation. It is whether the people of that nation acknowledge the authority of Jesus Christ.

A Christian political vision must take seriously both the particular responsibilities of civil government and the universal scope of the gospel. It must reject the notion that ethnicity confers spiritual rank while recognizing that Christ's authority extends over every people and every nation.

**No tribe outranks another before the throne of God, and no bloodline buys the mercy seat. Christ alone is King, and His kingdom gathers people from all nations.**
