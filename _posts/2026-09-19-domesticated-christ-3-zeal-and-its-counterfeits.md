*Part three of five. So far the work has been mostly descriptive: here is what Christ did, here is what the apostles did. This week the question sharpens. If Scripture commands zeal and does not merely permit it, then what matters is telling the genuine article from its imitations. There are imitations on both sides.*

## Anathema: Doctrinal Truth Worth Defending Fiercely

Galatians 1:6–9 turns the same apostolic seriousness toward doctrinal corruption rather than toward a man in the room. Paul is astonished, a word carrying the sense of stunned dismay, that the Galatians are so quickly deserting the one who called them by the grace of Christ for a different gospel, which he says is really no gospel at all. Then comes the sentence: "even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed." In case anyone took that for a flourish born of one frustrated moment, he repeats it in the next verse.

This is not the language of a man who treats doctrinal precision as a specialist's hobby, nor of a man whose first concern is giving no offense. Paul defends the purity of the gospel with the strongest words available to him, twice, because he understands what is at stake: the eternal welfare of the Galatian believers. A gospel corrupted at its root does not save. Love for people being led astray, in Paul's hands, does not look like silence. It looks like anathema.

## Never Lacking in Zeal

None of this means Scripture celebrates intensity for its own sake. It does not. But neither does it ask us to put the fire out. Paul writes in Romans 12:11, "Never be lacking in zeal, but keep your spiritual fervor, serving the Lord." He tells the Galatians it is good to be made zealous for a good purpose at all times, not only when he is present with them (Galatians 4:18). Titus 2:14 describes the purpose of Christ's redemption as producing a people who are "zealous for good works." In the New Testament vocabulary, zeal is not a youthful excess to be tolerated for a while and outgrown with sanctification. It is commanded, and we are told never to let it cool.

So the biblical pattern is not "less passion" or "more passion." It is passion aimed and governed rightly. Paul faults his former self for zeal that was not according to knowledge (Romans 10:2). The problem there was never the heat. It was the direction.

## Distinguishing the Counterfeits from the Real Thing

Sloppy thinking here does real spiritual harm, so let the distinctions stand plainly. Zeal is not anger. Boldness is not aggression. Confrontation is not cruelty. Conviction is not arrogance. Preaching with heat is not emotional instability. Run the list the other direction and it holds just as well: gentleness is not cowardice, self-control is not apathy, and faithfulness to Christ is not the same thing as religious respectability.

These pairs matter because our culture, inside the church as much as outside it, keeps collapsing one term into the other. Intensity becomes suspect by default; composure becomes virtuous by default. But Scripture does not grade speech by its decibel level. A man can raise his voice because sinful anger, wounded pride, or a hunger to dominate has hold of him, and that is sin, condemned plainly throughout Scripture (Proverbs 29:11; James 1:19–20; Ephesians 4:31). A man can also raise his voice because he is deeply convinced of the truth, urgently aware of what hangs on it, and consumed with love for God and neighbor. The volume settles nothing by itself. What governs the passion, and what end it serves, decides whether it is sin or sanctified fervor.

I want to be just as clear about the other danger, because none of this licenses Christians to start raising their voices at people. Scripture is emphatic. We are to speak the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15). The Lord's servant must not be quarrelsome but kind, able to teach, patiently enduring evil, correcting opponents with gentleness (2 Timothy 2:24–25). Our speech should be seasoned with grace (Colossians 4:6). Human anger, left to itself, does not produce the righteousness of God (James 1:20). Self-control sits among the fruit of the Spirit for a reason (Galatians 5:22–23); it is not optional, and it is not zeal's enemy but its necessary companion. The argument of this essay is not "be louder." It is that the biblical alternative to sinful anger is self-control, not apathy, and we have quietly swapped the one for the other.

Which brings us to the objection every Christian eventually hears, and the one that deserves the most careful answer in this series: *just be like Jesus.* **Next week: what imitating Christ does and does not mean.**
