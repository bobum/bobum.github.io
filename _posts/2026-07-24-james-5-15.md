I’ll start by saying that I am a cessationist through and through. I believe the miraculous sign gifts served a unique purpose in redemptive history: they authenticated Christ, His apostles, and the revelation God was giving to establish His church.

Just as a building only has one foundation, the Church was “built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets” (Ephesians 2:20). Once that foundation was laid, the extraordinary signs that accompanied it served their purpose. Today, God has given His church the completed Scriptures—the sufficient, inspired, and authoritative Word of God by which we know Him, grow in Him, and are equipped for every good work.

But regardless of where someone lands on the continuationist versus cessationist discussion, I believe we should all agree on one foundational principle:

**Scripture interprets Scripture.**

Because all Scripture is God-breathed (2 Timothy 3:16), it is perfectly unified and never contradicts itself. We should never build a doctrine from a single verse in isolation. Instead, we must understand every passage in light of the whole counsel of God.

Recently, after a thoughtful discussion with my wife about James 5:15, I spent some time digging into the passage. I have to admit, my initial reaction was one of surprise.

On the surface, James appears to provide what looks like a clear prescription for healing:

> “Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up.” — James 5:14–15

At first glance, it almost reads like a formula: call the elders, anoint with oil, pray in faith, and healing follows.

I found myself asking some honest questions. Had my wife uncovered the passage that seemed to support the faith-healing teaching she had been exposed to as a young girl? Was this the text that would challenge my long-held cessationist convictions?

Those are the moments where we have to be careful. It is easy to come to Scripture looking for it to confirm what we already believe. But the goal is not to make Scripture fit our theology—the goal is to allow Scripture to shape our theology.

So I went back to the passage, not trying to defend my position, but trying to understand what James was actually saying.

### The condition Scripture gives us for prayer

James 5:15 says:

> “The prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up.”

If this verse is read by itself, it can sound like a guaranteed promise that every believing prayer for healing will result in physical recovery.

But when I compared it with the rest of Scripture, I found another principle that helps bring clarity.

First John 5:14–15 says:

> “And this is the confidence that we have toward him, that if we ask anything according to his will he hears us.”

John does not weaken our confidence in prayer—he defines it.

Our confidence is not that God will always do exactly what we ask. Our confidence is that God hears us and answers according to His perfect wisdom, goodness, and will.

That condition is not a limitation on prayer. It is the foundation of prayer.

### James already established this principle

One of the things that stood out to me was that James himself had already taught this concept.

Earlier in his letter, James writes:

> “Instead you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that.’” — James 4:15

James recognizes that every plan, every desire, and every expectation exists under the sovereignty of God.

It would be difficult to conclude that only a few verses later James intended prayers for healing to somehow be exempt from that principle.

The prayer of faith is not faith that God is obligated to act according to our desired outcome. It is faith that trusts God completely, even when His answer is different than what we hoped.

### Jesus demonstrated this perfectly

Jesus Himself gives us the clearest example.

In the Garden of Gethsemane, facing the cross, He prayed:

> “Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done.” — Luke 22:42

If anyone ever prayed with perfect faith, it was Christ. Yet His prayer demonstrates that faith is not demanding a specific outcome from God.

Faith submits to God because it trusts God.

### The apostles understood this reality

The New Testament also shows that faithful believers were not promised freedom from every sickness.

Paul pleaded three times for God to remove his “thorn in the flesh,” but God's answer was:

> “My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.” — 2 Corinthians 12:9

Paul did not lack faith. God simply had a greater purpose.

Paul also advised Timothy regarding his frequent ailments (1 Timothy 5:23), left Trophimus sick at Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20), and described Epaphroditus as being so ill that he nearly died before God had mercy on him (Philippians 2:27).

Even in the apostolic era, faithful believers experienced sickness and suffering.

### Where I landed

My conclusion is that James 5:15 is not a formula that guarantees physical healing if the correct steps are followed.

Rather, it is a call for believers to pray with faith, to seek the Lord, and to entrust the sick to the One who always answers according to His wisdom, goodness, and sovereign will.

The lesson I walked away with is simple:

**Never build a doctrine on a single verse. Compare Scripture with Scripture.**

When we allow God's Word to interpret God's Word, we do not find contradiction. We find consistency, unity, and truth.

And sometimes, the most valuable moments in Bible study are the ones where Scripture challenges our assumptions and drives us back to a deeper trust in the God who wrote it.
