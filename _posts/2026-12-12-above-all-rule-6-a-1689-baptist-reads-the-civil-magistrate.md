## The Old Covenant, the New Covenant, and the Civil Magistrate: A 1689 Baptist Perspective

One thing I would have appreciated at the conference was a more direct examination of the *1689 London Baptist Confession of Faith* alongside the Westminster Confession. Much of the historical discussion focused on the Westminster tradition, its understanding of the civil magistrate, and the subsequent revisions to its language concerning church–state relations. Those are important discussions, but as a Reformed Baptist who subscribes to the 1689 Confession, I approach these questions from a distinct confessional starting point.

The 1689 Confession draws substantially from the Westminster Confession and the Savoy Declaration, but its Baptist revisions are not incidental. Its covenant theology, understanding of the New Covenant, and treatment of the civil magistrate deserve to be considered on their own terms.

Chapter 24, *Of the Civil Magistrate*, affirms that God has ordained civil authorities for His own glory and the public good, and that Christians may lawfully serve as magistrates. It also addresses the responsibility of civil authorities to maintain justice and peace according to the wholesome laws of each kingdom and commonwealth. Chapter 24 does not reproduce the Westminster Confession's original language assigning the magistrate responsibility for suppressing heresy or regulating the church's religious affairs.

That distinction matters. I believe Christ's authority over civil government is comprehensive, but I do not believe that acknowledging His authority requires us to collapse the church's jurisdiction into that of the state. The church has been given its own officers, ordinances, discipline, and mission. The civil magistrate has a distinct responsibility to administer civil justice. Neither institution should usurp the authority Christ has assigned to the other.

The more difficult question is how the civil magistrate's obligation to uphold justice should be informed by God's revealed law, particularly when we consider the relationship between the Old Covenant's civil polity and the New Covenant era.

I do not believe we can answer that question merely by asserting that ancient Israel's civil laws are either all directly applicable or wholly irrelevant to contemporary government. We must examine the nature of the law, its covenantal context, its fulfillment in Christ, and the New Testament's teaching concerning civil authority. That is serious theological work, not a question that can be settled by slogans.

My commitment to the 1689 Confession does not diminish my conviction that Christ is Lord over the nations. Rather, it requires me to think carefully about how that lordship should be expressed through the distinct institutions God has ordained. The confession affirms the divine appointment and legitimate responsibilities of civil magistrates; it does not, by that affirmation alone, settle every contemporary dispute about Christian nationalism, theonomy, or the precise application of Old Testament civil laws.

We should be able to affirm both that the state is accountable to Christ and that the state is not the church. We should be able to reject religious coercion without embracing the fiction that civil government can operate according to a morally neutral standard. And we should be able to discuss the public implications of biblical law without pretending that every question of its application has already been resolved.

That is one reason I would have welcomed a more explicit Baptist engagement with these issues at the conference. The 1689 Confession provides a valuable framework for considering the civil magistrate's responsibilities while maintaining a robust distinction between the church and civil government. For those of us who subscribe to it, the task is to reason carefully from that confessional foundation about the implications of Christ's lordship for civil justice in the New Covenant era.

## The Church's Public Witness and the Limits of State Power

Another presentation argued that the church must not be reduced to a national project or subordinated to the priorities of political power. The church is a transnational community whose allegiance to Christ transcends every earthly regime.

That is an important corrective to any political movement that treats Christianity as merely a means of national advancement.

The church's public witness should be grounded in Scripture, expressed through faithful worship and teaching, and demonstrated through care for neighbors and the vulnerable. It should speak truthfully about marriage, family, justice, human dignity, and the common good, even when that witness challenges prevailing political assumptions.

The church should not become an instrument of the state. Neither should the state be treated as a substitute for the church.

At the same time, the church's transnational identity does not make national identity meaningless, nor does it relieve civil rulers of their responsibility to govern justly. A Christian in America remains a member of the universal church while also living under the jurisdiction of a particular civil government.

The challenge is to preserve these distinctions without pretending that Christian faith has no public implications.

We must also recognize that a Christian society and a regenerate church are not identical. Laws cannot create faith, and outward conformity to Christian standards does not establish that a person has been born again. A nation might possess Christian institutions, traditions, and laws while remaining deeply compromised by hypocrisy and nominalism.

That is a reason to take the church's responsibility for discipleship and discipline seriously, not a reason to conclude that Christian principles should have no place in civil government.

The proper response to nominal Christianity is not to abandon the public claims of Christianity. It is to insist on genuine discipleship, faithful churches, moral integrity, and a proper understanding of the distinct responsibilities of ecclesiastical and civil authority.
