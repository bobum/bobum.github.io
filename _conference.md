# Above All Rule: Christ’s Authority over Government, the Gospel of the Kingdom, and the Question of Christian Nationalism

I recently attended a conference in Louisville, Kentucky, titled *Above All Rule: Christ’s Authority over Government*. The conference explored a question that Christians have wrestled with for centuries: What does it mean for Jesus Christ to possess all authority, not merely over the individual believer or the institutional church, but over the nations, their rulers, and the civil order itself?

The discussions were challenging, thoughtful, and at times sharply divergent. The speakers approached the subject from Baptist, Presbyterian, Reformed, and post-liberal perspectives, examining the historical relationship between church and state, the implications of the New Covenant, the role of civil magistrates, and the contemporary debate surrounding Christian nationalism.

I left with several things to consider, but also with some convictions reinforced. In particular, I believe we need to think more carefully about the relationship between the gospel of the kingdom, the Christian character of a society, and the civil government's responsibility under the lordship of Jesus Christ.

## 1. Christ's Authority Is Not Limited to the Private Lives of Christians

The conference's title establishes the central theological question: If Christ is above all rule and authority, what are the implications of that truth for government?

Christ's lordship is not a metaphor, nor is it a reality confined to the church's internal affairs. Jesus declared that all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to Him (Matthew 28:18). That declaration precedes the Great Commission, in which He commands His disciples to make disciples of all nations, baptizing them and teaching them to observe everything He commanded.

The scope of Christ's authority is universal. The question is not whether Christ rules over the nations, but how that authority should be understood and acknowledged in the ordering of human society.

The conference explored several answers to that question. Some speakers emphasized the distinction between the church's spiritual mission and the state's temporal responsibilities. Others argued that the church is a transnational community whose authority and public witness cannot be reduced to national identity or the priorities of the modern state. Still others examined the historical Reformed understanding that civil magistrates have positive responsibilities toward religion, including removing impediments to worship and, in some formulations, supporting the public practice of Christianity.

These distinctions matter. The church is not the state, and the state is not the church. The state cannot regenerate a heart, forgive sins, or mediate eternal life. Those are not functions of civil government.

But it does not follow that civil government is religiously neutral, that public life should be insulated from biblical morality, or that Christ's authority has no meaningful implications for civil law.

A government necessarily operates according to some understanding of justice, human nature, moral obligation, and the common good. The question is whether those commitments will be informed by the truth of God's revelation or by competing accounts of morality and human flourishing.

There is no genuinely neutral position on these questions. The disagreement concerns which moral framework should govern public life and how that framework should be expressed through civil authority.

## 2. The Gospel Is the Gospel of the Kingdom, Not Merely Personal Salvation

One of the most important distinctions we must recover is the difference between saying that the gospel includes personal salvation and saying that the gospel is exclusively about personal salvation.

Personal salvation is indispensable. We are sinners who need reconciliation with God through Jesus Christ. No discussion of the kingdom can diminish the necessity of repentance, faith, forgiveness, justification, and regeneration.

But the biblical gospel is larger than the individual experience of conversion.

Jesus proclaimed the gospel of the kingdom. He announced the arrival of God's reign, called people to repentance, taught His disciples how to live under His authority, and commissioned them to disciple the nations. The apostles proclaimed Christ crucified and risen, but they also announced His enthronement, His present reign, and His coming judgment of the world.

Consider the Lord's Prayer:

*“Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven.”* (Matthew 6:10, KJV)

This is not merely a prayer for individuals to experience personal salvation. It is a prayer that God's reign would be manifested, that His will would be done on earth, and that earthly life would increasingly conform to heavenly realities.

Consider the Great Commission. Jesus does not command His disciples merely to seek individual conversions and leave the nations' collective life untouched. He commands them to make disciples of all nations and to teach those disciples to observe everything He has commanded.

The nations are within the scope of the commission. So is obedience to Christ's teaching.

This does not mean that every aspect of the Great Commission is a direct mandate for civil legislation. The church makes disciples through the proclamation of the gospel, baptism, teaching, and discipleship—not through the coercive power of the state. Nevertheless, the commission gives us no warrant to restrict Christ's claims to the private sphere.

The gospel announces a King. It announces a kingdom. And it calls people, communities, and ultimately the nations to reckon with His authority.

If our understanding of the gospel terminates at the salvation of isolated individuals, we risk losing the biblical vision of Christ's comprehensive reign. Personal salvation is essential, but it is not the entire horizon of the kingdom.

## 3. Eschatology Matters: Are We Building, or Are We Waiting to Leave?

One of the speakers made an observation that I found particularly significant: our eschatology shapes how we approach nationalism, government, and the Christian's responsibility in public life.

I agree. What we believe about the future of Christ's kingdom inevitably influences what we believe Christians should be doing in the present.

I approach this question as a postmillennialist. I believe Christ reigns now, that the Great Commission will accomplish what He intends it to accomplish, and that the gospel will have a profound and extensive effect on the nations before Christ returns. I do not expect the church's historical trajectory to be one of inevitable worldwide defeat, with Christian faithfulness consisting primarily of holding on until we are finally removed from the scene.

That conviction fundamentally shapes how I think about Christian engagement with society.

If we believe the gospel will progressively transform peoples and nations, then Christian political and cultural engagement is not an embarrassing distraction from the real work of the church. It is one of the areas in which the consequences of discipleship must be considered. Christians should be thinking about what faithful obedience looks like in government, law, education, family life, economics, and every other sphere of human responsibility.

The Great Commission is not a command to retreat from the world while waiting for an escape. It is a command to make disciples of all nations and teach them to obey Christ. The Lord's Prayer does not teach us to ask that God's will be done on earth only until things become sufficiently bad for us to leave. It teaches us to pray for His kingdom to come and His will to be done on earth as it is in heaven.

This is where I find myself sharply at odds with forms of eschatological thinking that encourage Christians to view cultural decline as inevitable, political faithfulness as largely futile, and the rapture as the ultimate solution to the world's problems.

If we have already decided that the nations must inevitably descend into greater rebellion until Christ removes His people, it becomes difficult to sustain a compelling vision for long-term Christian institution-building. Why invest in the transformation of a society if we fundamentally expect that society to deteriorate beyond recovery? Why labor for the discipling of nations if our practical expectation is that the nations will remain comprehensively hostile to Christ until the very end?

I recognize that not every premillennialist reasons this way. Many work faithfully in their churches, communities, and professions, and many affirm the importance of evangelism, discipleship, and public service. Nor does belief in a future rapture, by itself, establish that someone is indifferent to the present world.

My criticism is directed at the fatalism that can accompany popular rapture-centered eschatology: the expectation that the world must grow progressively worse, that the nations will ultimately remain hostile to Christ, and that the church's ultimate hope lies in being removed from the world rather than in the gospel's progressive work of discipling the nations. When that expectation becomes the lens through which Christians interpret history, cultural decline can become something to anticipate rather than resist. Defeat becomes the expected outcome, retreat becomes easier to justify, and long-term investment in Christian institutions can seem almost futile. I believe that outlook deserves a serious theological challenge. If Christ has commissioned us to disciple the nations, we should be very cautious about adopting an eschatology that leads us to expect the enduring failure of that commission at the level of nations and cultures before He returns.

The hope of Christ's return should produce faithfulness, not passivity. The certainty of final victory should strengthen our labor, not make it seem pointless. And the promise that Christ will consummate His kingdom should encourage us to work in the confidence that our labor in the Lord is not in vain.

Postmillennialism does not promise that every political campaign will succeed, that every generation will be more righteous than the last, or that Christians can usher in the kingdom through political power. It does, however, give us a reason to expect that the gospel can accomplish far more in history than merely preserving a remnant until the end.

I want Christians to think in terms of generations, not simply escape plans. I want us to build institutions, educate our children, disciple our communities, pursue justice, and consider what it would mean for entire societies to order their lives under the lordship of Christ.

We should not be preoccupied with predicting the date of our departure when Christ has given us work to do.

**The question is not merely whether we are ready for Christ to return. It is whether we are faithfully carrying out the work He has commanded us to do while we wait.**

## 4. Christian Nationalism and the Christian Character of a Nation

The conference included substantial criticism of Christian nationalism. Some of those concerns deserve serious consideration.

There is a legitimate danger in confusing national identity with Christian identity, treating the nation as though it were the church, or using Christianity as a tool for political ambition. The church is composed of believers from every tribe, tongue, people, and nation. No earthly nation can claim to exhaust the identity of the people of God.

There are also legitimate concerns about coercion, religious liberty, nominal Christianity, and the temptation to use state power to accomplish what only the Holy Spirit can accomplish.

However, I believe we must distinguish between these dangers and the broader question of whether a nation can, or should, order its public life according to Christian principles. The fact that Christian nationalism can be distorted or abused does not, by itself, establish that a nation should refuse to acknowledge Christ or that Christian principles should be excluded from civil government.

Christian nationalism is a term used to describe a variety of positions, and those positions should not all be treated as interchangeable. Ethnic nationalism dressed in Christian language is not the same thing as a conviction that Jesus Christ is Lord over civil rulers and that His moral law is relevant to public justice.

Nor is the belief that a nation should acknowledge Christ necessarily a belief that the church and state should become one institution.

My own position begins with a relatively straightforward observation: **If a population becomes substantially Christian in its convictions, worldview, and practice, it is entirely natural that its laws and institutions will increasingly reflect those convictions.** I would go further: I believe a society ought to evaluate its public life according to the truth of God's revelation rather than treating Christianity as relevant only to private belief and personal conduct.

Laws are not created in a cultural vacuum. They reflect the moral assumptions, priorities, and beliefs of the people who make them. A society that comes to embrace Christianity deeply and broadly will inevitably begin to express that transformation in its understanding of justice, family, education, public morality, and civil responsibility.

If a population is significantly Christian and seeks to live righteously, why would we expect its public institutions to remain fundamentally unchanged? Why would we expect a Christian majority to regard its deepest convictions as relevant to personal conduct but irrelevant to the collective life of the nation?

This does not mean that whatever a Christian majority supports is automatically just, or that every Christian conviction translates directly into a civil statute. Christian political judgments must themselves be tested against Scripture, and the proper scope of civil authority must be considered carefully. Nor can a government manufacture genuine Christianity through legislation. It cannot.

Rather, my argument is that a society shaped by Christianity will naturally have political consequences, and that a society ought not to regard those consequences as illegitimate merely because they arise from Christian convictions. A society shaped by Christianity will not make the same moral and political judgments as a society shaped by secular humanism, materialism, or another competing worldview.

The question is not whether a society's dominant worldview will influence its laws. It will. The deeper question is which worldview ought to shape them, and by what standard its laws should be judged. I believe that standard must ultimately be grounded in God's truth, with civil government exercising its authority within the responsibilities God has assigned to it.

## 5. Christian Nationalism Is Not Ethnic Supremacy

One objection to Christian nationalism is that it can become entangled with ethnic nationalism, treating a particular ancestry as though it confers a superior claim to a nation or to God's favor. That danger is real, but it is not inherent in the conviction that a nation should acknowledge Christ.

I do not believe any tribe possesses an inherent spiritual superiority over another. No bloodline purchases a place before God, and no ancestry gives anyone a claim on the mercy seat. We are all sinners in need of the same grace, and salvation is found in Jesus Christ rather than in our genealogy.

The church itself testifies to this reality. Christ is gathering a people from every tribe, tongue, people, and nation. Our unity in Him does not depend on shared ethnicity, and no earthly ancestry places someone closer to the throne of grace.

That conviction should shape how Christians think about the nation. If a nation is to be Christian, its defining characteristic should be its acknowledgment of Christ and its commitment to ordering public life according to Christian convictions—not the supposed superiority of a particular bloodline.

This does not mean that national borders, citizenship, cultural inheritance, or a government's responsibility toward its own population are meaningless. Those are legitimate subjects for political and theological discussion. But they must not be turned into claims of racial superiority or hereditary spiritual privilege.

Christianity cannot be reduced to an ethnic inheritance. One can possess a Christian ancestry without possessing Christian faith, just as someone from a people with no historical Christian majority can be brought into the kingdom through the gospel.

The relevant question is not which bloodline has the strongest claim to a nation. It is whether the people of that nation acknowledge the authority of Jesus Christ.

A Christian political vision must take seriously both the particular responsibilities of civil government and the universal scope of the gospel. It must reject the notion that ethnicity confers spiritual rank while recognizing that Christ's authority extends over every people and every nation.

**No tribe outranks another before the throne of God, and no bloodline buys the mercy seat. Christ alone is King, and His kingdom gathers people from all nations.**

## 6. Theonomy and Christian Nationalism: A Relationship Worth Clarifying

Another conviction I brought away from the conference concerns the relationship between theonomy and Christian nationalism.

Theonomy, broadly understood, holds that God's law provides the ultimate standard of justice and that civil government is not exempt from God's moral authority. The precise application of Old Testament law to contemporary civil government is a substantial theological question, and theonomists themselves do not agree on every detail.

Christian nationalism, likewise, has multiple definitions. For purposes of my argument, I am using the term to describe the conviction that a nation should acknowledge its Christian character and order its public life, including its civil institutions, in recognition of Christ's authority.

With those definitions in view, I believe that **theonomy requires a Christian-national framework if it is to become the governing principle of civil law.**

By this, I mean a comprehensive theonomic vision in which God's revealed law is acknowledged as the authoritative standard for civil justice—not merely a political order that happens to enact individual laws consistent with biblical morality. Theonomy makes a claim about the standard by which civil law should be measured. If God's law is to serve as that governing standard, the political community must, in some meaningful sense, acknowledge the authority of the God who gave it.

A society that formally excludes Christianity from its understanding of public authority cannot consistently implement a comprehensive theonomic vision. It may adopt individual laws that happen to agree with biblical standards, but that is different from recognizing God's law as the authoritative standard for civil justice.

In that sense, I see Christian nationalism, as I have defined it here, as the political framework within which comprehensive theonomy could be pursued. This does not mean that every form of Christian nationalism is theonomic, that every Christian-nationalist position agrees on the application of biblical law, or that the label itself resolves the theological questions involved.

This does not settle the difficult questions. Which Old Testament laws continue to apply to civil government? How should we distinguish enduring moral principles from laws tied specifically to Israel's covenantal polity? What role should the New Covenant play in interpreting those laws? How should civil authority be exercised without confusing the magistrate's responsibilities with those of the church?

These are not peripheral details. They are central theological questions that require careful biblical and confessional reasoning.

But the difficulty of answering them does not eliminate the underlying issue: If Christ is Lord over civil government, then civil government must be accountable to a standard beyond its own will. Theonomy attempts to identify that standard in God's revealed law. Whether a particular theonomic proposal faithfully identifies and applies that law must be established through careful exegesis and theological argument, not assumed merely because the proposal invokes Scripture.

## 7. The Old Covenant, the New Covenant, and the Civil Magistrate: A 1689 Baptist Perspective

One thing I would have appreciated at the conference was a more direct examination of the *1689 London Baptist Confession of Faith* alongside the Westminster Confession. Much of the historical discussion focused on the Westminster tradition, its understanding of the civil magistrate, and the subsequent revisions to its language concerning church–state relations. Those are important discussions, but as a Reformed Baptist who subscribes to the 1689 Confession, I approach these questions from a distinct confessional starting point.

The 1689 Confession draws substantially from the Westminster Confession and the Savoy Declaration, but its Baptist revisions are not incidental. Its covenant theology, understanding of the New Covenant, and treatment of the civil magistrate deserve to be considered on their own terms.

Chapter 24, *Of the Civil Magistrate*, affirms that God has ordained civil authorities for His own glory and the public good, and that Christians may lawfully serve as magistrates. It also addresses the responsibility of civil authorities to maintain justice and peace according to the wholesome laws of each kingdom and commonwealth. Chapter 24 does not reproduce the Westminster Confession's original language assigning the magistrate responsibility for suppressing heresy or regulating the church's religious affairs.

That distinction matters. I believe Christ's authority over civil government is comprehensive, but I do not believe that acknowledging His authority requires us to collapse the church's jurisdiction into that of the state. The church has been given its own officers, ordinances, discipline, and mission. The civil magistrate has a distinct responsibility to administer civil justice. Neither institution should usurp the authority Christ has assigned to the other.

The more difficult question is how the civil magistrate's obligation to uphold justice should be informed by God's revealed law, particularly when we consider the relationship between the Old Covenant's civil polity and the New Covenant era.

I do not believe we can answer that question merely by asserting that ancient Israel's civil laws are either all directly applicable or wholly irrelevant to contemporary government. We must examine the nature of the law, its covenantal context, its fulfillment in Christ, and the New Testament's teaching concerning civil authority. That is serious theological work, not a question that can be settled by slogans.

My commitment to the 1689 Confession does not diminish my conviction that Christ is Lord over the nations. Rather, it requires me to think carefully about how that lordship should be expressed through the distinct institutions God has ordained. The confession affirms the divine appointment and legitimate responsibilities of civil magistrates; it does not, by that affirmation alone, settle every contemporary dispute about Christian nationalism, theonomy, or the precise application of Old Testament civil laws.

We should be able to affirm both that the state is accountable to Christ and that the state is not the church. We should be able to reject religious coercion without embracing the fiction that civil government can operate according to a morally neutral standard. And we should be able to discuss the public implications of biblical law without pretending that every question of its application has already been resolved.

That is one reason I would have welcomed a more explicit Baptist engagement with these issues at the conference. The 1689 Confession provides a valuable framework for considering the civil magistrate's responsibilities while maintaining a robust distinction between the church and civil government. For those of us who subscribe to it, the task is to reason carefully from that confessional foundation about the implications of Christ's lordship for civil justice in the New Covenant era.

## 8. The Church's Public Witness and the Limits of State Power

Another presentation argued that the church must not be reduced to a national project or subordinated to the priorities of political power. The church is a transnational community whose allegiance to Christ transcends every earthly regime.

That is an important corrective to any political movement that treats Christianity as merely a means of national advancement.

The church's public witness should be grounded in Scripture, expressed through faithful worship and teaching, and demonstrated through care for neighbors and the vulnerable. It should speak truthfully about marriage, family, justice, human dignity, and the common good, even when that witness challenges prevailing political assumptions.

The church should not become an instrument of the state. Neither should the state be treated as a substitute for the church.

At the same time, the church's transnational identity does not make national identity meaningless, nor does it relieve civil rulers of their responsibility to govern justly. A Christian in America remains a member of the universal church while also living under the jurisdiction of a particular civil government.

The challenge is to preserve these distinctions without pretending that Christian faith has no public implications.

We must also recognize that a Christian society and a regenerate church are not identical. Laws cannot create faith, and outward conformity to Christian standards does not establish that a person has been born again. A nation might possess Christian institutions, traditions, and laws while remaining deeply compromised by hypocrisy and nominalism.

That is a reason to take the church's responsibility for discipleship and discipline seriously, not a reason to conclude that Christian principles should have no place in civil government.

The proper response to nominal Christianity is not to abandon the public claims of Christianity. It is to insist on genuine discipleship, faithful churches, moral integrity, and a proper understanding of the distinct responsibilities of ecclesiastical and civil authority.

## 9. What Would It Mean for a Nation to Acknowledge Christ?

This is where the discussion becomes practical.

If we confess that Christ is King of kings and Lord of lords, we must consider what that confession means beyond the walls of the church. What does it mean for a civil magistrate to acknowledge that he is accountable to Christ? What does it mean for a society to recognize that justice is not defined merely by majority preference, legislative decree, or contemporary cultural consensus?

These questions cannot be answered simply by placing Christian symbols on government buildings or invoking God in political speeches. A genuinely Christian public order would have to reflect a substantive understanding of biblical justice, human responsibility, moral accountability, and the common good.

It would also require Christians to think carefully about the difference between persuading people of the truth and compelling outward conformity. Civil laws necessarily compel certain behaviors, but they cannot produce saving faith. The civil government's legitimate authority must be distinguished from the church's spiritual authority, and the limits of each must be taken seriously.

The conference rightly raised concerns about religious liberty, coercion, pluralism, and the dangers of conflating the church with the state. Those concerns deserve answers, not dismissal.

But neither should those concerns become a means of excluding Christianity from meaningful participation in public life.

If Christ possesses all authority, then no area of human life is ultimately outside His lordship. Civil government is not sovereign in an absolute sense. Neither is the individual, the nation, the church as a human institution, or any political ideology.

All are accountable to Christ.

## Conclusion: Above All Rule

I appreciated the opportunity to hear these different perspectives in Louisville. The discussions reminded me that the relationship between Christianity, civil government, and the nation is not a simplistic question, and that faithful Christians have reached different conclusions about how Christ's authority should be reflected in public institutions.

Nevertheless, I remain convinced that we must resist the tendency to reduce Christianity to a private religious experience or the gospel to personal salvation alone.

The gospel announces the reign of Jesus Christ. The Great Commission calls the church to make disciples of all nations and teach them to obey Christ's commands. The Lord's Prayer teaches us to pray for God's kingdom to come and His will to be done on earth as it is in heaven. Scripture declares that the rulers of the earth are accountable to the King whom God has established.

These are not merely private or individual concerns. They have implications for families, churches, communities, cultures, and nations.

A Christian population will naturally seek to express its convictions in its public life. If a society becomes substantially Christian in its beliefs and practices, it should not be surprising when its laws and institutions increasingly reflect those beliefs. And if God's law is to serve as the authoritative standard for civil justice, then a political order that acknowledges Christ's authority provides the necessary framework for that vision.

The difficult work is determining how these convictions should be applied faithfully under the New Covenant, while preserving the distinction between church and state and refusing to confuse civil conformity with saving faith.

We should neither retreat from the public implications of the gospel nor assume that political power can accomplish the work of regeneration.

Our task is to proclaim Christ faithfully, make disciples diligently, teach the whole counsel of God, and think seriously about what it means to live under His authority in every sphere of life.

Christ does not merely offer salvation to individuals. He is the risen and reigning King.

As a postmillennialist, I believe we have every reason and responsibility to labor for generations we may never see, building faithfully on the conviction that Christ's kingdom will advance and that our work in the Lord is not in vain. We do not know every detail of how that advance will unfold, and we cannot bring it about through political power. But neither should we presume that the transformation of nations is beyond the reach of the gospel. We have been given a commission, a King who possesses all authority, and a promise that His purposes will not fail.

**He is above all rule and authority. The question is whether we are willing to take the full implications of that truth seriously.**
