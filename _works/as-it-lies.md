back_cover_text: |
  *As It Lies* follows Dr. Alister MacKenzie from a freezing field hospital on the Western Front to a sun-struck Georgia nursery that would become Augusta National. He begins as a surgeon watching a General order a trench cut dead straight across an exposed ridge, while an older trench — one that simply followed the lie of the land — quietly saves every man in it. That lesson never leaves him: you do not impose a shape on the ground. You find the one already there.

  He carries it out of two wars and into a profession that barely exists yet. It costs him a marriage to a woman who calls his life's work a hobby, a fortune he never manages to hold, and the patience of clients who want their hazards obvious and their fees negotiable. It gains him a second wife who listens, a scruffy terrier he calls the real surveyor, and a sixteen-year-old caddy with a spiral notebook who writes down everything the old man says without understanding a word of it until much later.

  This is a novel about authorship and erasure — about the difference between building a monument and letting a place speak. MacKenzie died on the sixth of January, 1934, in a rented house above a fairway he could no longer afford, with the last of his design fee unpaid and the first Masters ten weeks away. The ground he shaped is now the most photographed landscape in sport. Almost no one who walks it can spell his name.

Dr. Alister MacKenzie was a Yorkshire physician with Highland blood, a Boer War surgeon who came home fascinated by the wrong thing. While the Army studied Boer marksmanship, he studied Boer *concealment* — earthworks so faithful to the surrounding country that artillery could not find them. In the trenches of France he made a career of it, teaching the British Army to hide what it built. And somewhere between the mud and the drawing board he understood that the discipline had a second use: a golf hole, like a gun emplacement, should look as though no one put it there at all.

*As It Lies* traces the thirty years that idea took to become Augusta National. It moves between the Leeds parlor where Edith balances the household ledgers in flawless copperplate and informs him that no one needs golf courses; the fog-soaked fairways of Pasatiempo where a caddy named Louie learns to read ground he will never be permitted to play; the Monterey cypress at Cypress Point; and a shuttered Georgia nursery where Bobby Jones — the only golfer MacKenzie ever envied — has an idea he cannot draw, and Clifford Roberts has a chequebook he intends to keep closed.

The story is told largely through witnesses, because that is the shape of the man's life: an architect whose signature is invisible by design. Louie sees him at his most unguarded and least explicable, a stooped figure in a holed tweed jacket and a plaid kilt arguing with a hillside. Hilda sees the cost of the arguing. Roberts sees an invoice. Jones sees, briefly and completely, exactly what MacKenzie sees — and that recognition is the closest thing to friendship the book allows either of them.

It ends where it must. A telegram. A last sketch. A bedroom smelling of camphor above a fairway in January, and a man writing *poor house* in his own hand in a letter that gets no reply inside the year. Then a spring afternoon two years on, a boy in a clearing with a soft-cornered notebook, and four hundred people making a single noise because the ground asked them to.
