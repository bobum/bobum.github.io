It begins on a freezing ridge in France with a surgeon's hands still black with another man's blood. Dr. Alister MacKenzie watches a work detail cut a new trench exactly as the General's chart demands — dead straight, rigid, undoubting, across the shallowest part of the ridge and directly into the German field of fire. A hundred yards to the left, an older trench curls through a stand of hawthorn, following the natural lie of the ground, invisible from the far guns. One of them will fill his operating table by morning. The other has been quietly saving lives for a year. MacKenzie has already survived one war learning how the Boers hid earthworks the artillery could never find; here, in the mud, the principle finishes forming. Do not impose a shape on the ground. Find the one that is already there.

Thirty years later that idea has cost him nearly everything. It cost him Edith, who kept immaculate ledgers and told him plainly that no one needs golf courses. It cost him a physician's income, a Leeds practice, and a series of fees he was too proud to chase and too broke to forgive. What it bought him was a reputation — Alwoodley, Moortown, Cypress Point, Royal Melbourne, Pasatiempo — and a strange, hard-won peace on the California coast with Hilda, who listens to his drawings instead of dismissing them, a terrier named Mick he calls the real surveyor, and a sixteen-year-old caddy named Louie who carries his bag at six-thirty every morning and writes down the things he says.

Then a letter arrives from Georgia. Robert T. Jones, Jr. and Clifford Roberts have three hundred and sixty-five acres of abandoned nursery and an idea neither of them can draw. What follows is the last and best argument of MacKenzie's life: a running two-year negotiation with Jones over strategy and width and the courage to leave ground alone, and a colder one with Roberts over bunkers, yardage, and money that never quite arrives. He wins the argument. He loses almost everything else. On the sixth of January, 1934, in a rented house above a Pasatiempo fairway, with twenty-five hundred dollars of his design fee still outstanding and the first Masters ten weeks away, the ground stops speaking to him.

*As It Lies* is told largely by the people who watched — most of all by Louie, who did not understand what he was recording until two springs later, standing in a clearing at Augusta with a soft-cornered notebook, listening to four hundred people make a single noise because the ground had asked them to.
