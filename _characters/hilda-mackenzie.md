Hilda Sykes Haddock enters the novel quietly and changes its temperature. Born in Mirfield in 1874, widowed by the violin collector Edgar Augustus Haddock, and mother to a grown son the family calls Tony, she is not young, not impressed, and not looking to be rescued. She arrives at MacKenzie's study while he is mid-explanation and does the one thing nobody has done in twenty-five years: she pays attention.

The novel makes the contrast unmistakable and refuses to make it sentimental. Where Edith stood framed in the archway as a spectator, Hilda comes into the room. She watches his hands rather than the drawings. She takes the kettle off the cold side of the stove and sets it to boil without asking and without comment, the way a woman restocks a fire — because the room requires it. She refills the tea caddy from a tin in her own coat pocket, empty since January, loose-leaf and strong. Her finger rests *near* the penciled curve of a creek and never on it, a quarter-inch gap maintained from the first visit onward: an unspoken respect for the surface of another person's thinking. *Edith had dismissed what she failed to grasp. Hilda catalogued it.* Between dismissal and cataloguing, the book observes, lies the distance between two marriages.

She marries him in London in May 1930, weeks after the Reno divorce is finalized, and goes with him to California. She gets three and a half years. They are the years of Augusta, which means they are years of travel, worry, unpaid invoices, and a heart that is quietly failing. She is the one who understands what the money trouble actually means and the one who cannot fix it. On the sixth of January, 1934, she brings him lunch at the house above the Pasatiempo fairway. She outlives him by nearly nine years and dies in Leeds in 1942.
