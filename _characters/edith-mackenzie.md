Edith Mary Wedderburn married Alister MacKenzie in Leeds in 1905 and spent the next quarter-century being right about the money. That is the tragedy of her, and the novel is careful not to caricature it. She is not stupid, not cruel, and not wrong about the household accounts. She is simply incapable of granting that the thing consuming her husband is a thing at all.

She appears first as a silhouette in an archway, one hand on the threshold — the posture of a spectator, which is the only role she ever occupies in his working life. Her profile is sharp in the gaslight and could have belonged to any of the Wedderburn women: a family of noses and chins and the moral certainty of rectitude. She keeps the ledgers in flawless copperplate. She waits at the threshold while he spreads plans across the far side of the table and never crosses it. Her lip curls, almost imperceptibly, at the word *strategy*. Disappointment is worn like a stole, draped for effect; if she is tired she conceals it behind good posture and clean cuffs.

Her judgment is delivered in four words and never withdrawn: *No one needs golf courses.* It becomes the sentence MacKenzie hears every time the pencil returns to paper, her face swimming up with her lips pressed into a bloodless seam, the line wavering like a man walking against too strong a wind. It follows him to Cypress Point and to Georgia. It is still audible in the last chapters, when the accounts prove her point and he keeps drawing anyway. MacKenzie filed for divorce in Reno in November 1929, alleging desertion; it was granted in January 1930. Edith remained at Moor Allerton Lodge in Leeds, where she died on the eighth of July, 1939, five years after the man she could not understand had been declared a genius by people who never had to balance his books.
