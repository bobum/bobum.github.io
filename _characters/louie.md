Louie is the novel's witness, and he does not know it. He is sixteen, Mexican-American on his mother's Californio side, and he waits under the eucalyptus by the third tee at six o'clock every morning with his chin on the strap of a canvas golf bag heavier than he is. His forearms are baked darker than the rest of him from years of loop work. His father mows tee boxes for the maintenance crew on Sundays and mutters prayers to La Virgen when the sprinklers break. The number forty-three is inked onto his thumb pad most mornings before the ink is dry on the pad itself.

He will never be permitted to play the course he can read better than the members. That fact sits in the book without complaint and without apology. What he has instead is a spiral notebook with a cardboard cover, in which he keeps shot charts, club preferences, wind notes, and — increasingly — the things Dr. MacKenzie says to nobody in particular while staring at a hillside. *It's not a hazard. It's a decision.* Louie writes it down because it sounds important, not because he understands it. That gap between transcription and comprehension is the whole architecture of his character.

MacKenzie calls him Louis, biting off the last syllable as though unsure which part matters, and treats him with the specific, unsentimental respect of a man who has decided that this boy sees ground properly. It is the closest thing to an apprenticeship either of them will admit to. Louie is present for the drawings, the arguments, the money worries he is too young to be hearing, and finally the camphor-smelling bedroom above the fairway in January. He is the one who reads the obituary. And two springs later, standing in a clearing at Augusta among four hundred people making one noise because the ground asked them to, he takes the pen from behind his ear and writes the last line in full, because the shorthand is not enough for that one: *The acid test of a golf course is its abiding popularity.*
