Mick is a small wire-haired terrier and the least complicated relationship in the book. He walks the property before MacKenzie does — nose low to the boards, tail high, leading the way as though the route belongs to him. He runs the perimeter of the caddy shack in a circuit as thorough as any architect's survey. He arrives at the study first, stops at the shoe, cocks his head at the paper, and waits.

The joke MacKenzie makes on first introducing him to Louie is not entirely a joke. *This is Mick. You know Mick. The real surveyor.* A man who has spent his life insisting that the ground already knows its own shape has an obvious professional respect for an animal that reads terrain with its nose and stops, ears forward, at exactly the junction where a path wants to go. When MacKenzie pauses on a rise to let the terrier sniff at a molehill, both of them cock their heads at the same moment, listening for something underneath. It is the single clearest image of what the man actually does for a living.

He is also the novel's emotional barometer, and it uses him sparingly and without mercy. Mick sits at the heel when MacKenzie stops walking. His ears flatten when the conversation turns to money. He waits at the base of a grading stake in the long bars of late shadow while the yardage argument goes on above him. And at the very end, two springs after the house above the Pasatiempo fairway has gone quiet, it is Louie who shuts the notebook, puts it in his back pocket where it belongs, and says *Mick. Heel.*
