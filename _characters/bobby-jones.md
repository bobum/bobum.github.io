Bobby Jones arrives in the novel as a signature on a letter, and the name carries its own weather system. He is the golden child of the age: the Georgia Tech engineer and Harvard man who won the U.S. Open, U.S. Amateur, British Open and British Amateur in a single calendar year and then walked away from competition at twenty-eight while still the best player on earth. To MacKenzie — a man who chops at the ball with stiff arms and locked legs, a swing closer to working a knot out of timber than to any professional's arc — Jones is the only golfer who ever provoked envy rather than analysis.

What makes the partnership work is that Jones is not a client. He is a collaborator with a specific and unusual humility: he knows exactly what he wants the ground to ask a player, and he knows he has no ability whatsoever to put it on paper. He wants width. He wants options. He wants a course that punishes ambition rather than error, that a member can enjoy on Sunday and a champion can lose on Thursday. MacKenzie has spent thirty years building the vocabulary for precisely that argument and has never once had a client who already spoke it.

Their alliance is the warmest thing in the book, and also the most restrained — two courteous, self-contained men who mostly discuss dirt. Jones supplies legitimacy, vision, and the golfer's authority to overrule objections; MacKenzie supplies the routing, the contours, and the professional nerve to leave a hazard out. Jones is famous for calling penalties on himself that no official saw, and the novel takes that seriously: he is genuinely decent, and genuinely unable to fix the thing that matters most. He is not the one who holds the chequebook. When the last of MacKenzie's fee goes unpaid and the architect writes *poor house* in his own hand, Jones is the public face of a club that has no money, and the failure is structural rather than personal — which does not make it hurt any less.
