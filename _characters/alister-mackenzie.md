Alister MacKenzie is a physician who stopped believing in medicine and a soldier who never stopped believing in cover. Born in Normanton to a Highland doctor, christened Alexander and called Alister, he qualified at Cambridge and Leeds and joined his father's practice — and then went to South Africa as a civilian surgeon and spent the war watching the wrong thing. While the Army catalogued Boer marksmanship, MacKenzie catalogued Boer concealment: firing positions and earthworks shaped so faithfully to the surrounding country that British guns could not find them to answer. He came home with a conviction he could not yet name, and the Western Front gave him the language for it. Transferred out of surgery and into the Royal Engineers, he taught the British Army to build things that looked as though nobody had built them.

The transfer to golf is, to him, not a career change but the same sentence spoken in a friendlier country. A bunker is a gun emplacement. A green is dead ground. A hole that announces its own difficulty is a trench cut straight across a ridge because a chart said so. He is stubborn about this to the point of self-harm — he will spend an afternoon arguing with a hillside and a fortnight arguing with a committee, and he will lose money on both. In the field he is a stooped, energetic figure in a tweed cap and a jacket holed at both elbows, sometimes a plaid kilt, always with a terrier at his heel, rounding six-thirty down to six because it makes the day longer.

What the idea costs him is the spine of the novel. It costs him Edith, who keeps the household books in flawless copperplate and regards his drawings as an expensive tantrum. It costs him his practice, his savings, and a run of unpaid invoices he is too proud to litigate. It gives him Cypress Point, Royal Melbourne, Pasatiempo, and finally three hundred and sixty-five acres of Georgia nursery where Bobby Jones turns out to see precisely what he sees. He wins that argument completely. He does not get paid for it. He dies at Pasatiempo on the sixth of January, 1934, sixty-three years old, effectively penniless, having written the words *poor house* in his own hand and received no reply — ten weeks before the first Masters was played on his ground, and long before anyone thought to call him one of the great ones.
