Clifford Roberts is the counterweight to every romantic impulse in the novel. Where Jones supplies vision and MacKenzie supplies conviction, Roberts supplies the arithmetic — and the arithmetic does not care what the ground is saying. An Iowa-born investment dealer who made his career on Wall Street, he identified the old Fruitland Nurseries property, persuaded Jones to look at it, and then spent the next several years assembling the membership, the financing, the construction logistics, and the discipline that would turn three hundred and sixty-five acres of overgrown Georgia scrub into an institution.

He is not a villain, which is what makes him difficult. He is austere, watchful, formal, and almost entirely without theatre; his characteristic expression is not anger but controlled severity, the face of a man auditing everything in his field of view. His watch comes out of the vest pocket, gets checked, and returns in a single practiced motion. When the scale of the property first opens up in front of him, he lets out a soft whistle — quiet enough to deny later. That instinct to leave himself deniable is the whole man.

His conflict with MacKenzie is the slow, grinding kind: bunkers that cost money to build and money to rake, yardage that cost money to acquire, revisions that cost money to grade, and a design fee that keeps not arriving. The club is genuinely broke in those years, and Roberts is genuinely doing his job — protecting an enterprise that half the membership has not yet paid to join. But the ledger has a human cost, and the novel refuses to look away from it. MacKenzie agreed to a ten-thousand-dollar fee. He received roughly seven and a half. He died on the sixth of January, 1934, with the balance outstanding, having written to ask for it and received no reply inside the year. Roberts went on to chair Augusta National and the Masters for the better part of four decades.
